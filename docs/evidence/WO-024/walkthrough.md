Reading the name that the author put after the dash tells you what was checked. The test only followed two links to their targets. You need the assignment to learn whether anyone also promised to update the setup instructions. The fictional guard and check entries then show how different records can answer different questions about one afternoon.

**Strange loop.** The first instruction directs your eyes to the room. The second directs you back to the card on which you read it. In the gallery example, the visitor looks at a print of a seaport. Following the depicted buildings brings you back to the gallery containing the print. The return crosses the boundary between a picture and the place where someone looks at it.

**Collaboration.** Checkout and Release Platform agree on how a preview should work. A new Checkout engineer then uses the instructions to create a preview, identify a failed build and remove the preview independently. That is the evidence they agreed would let them end joint sessions. Reaching the ten-day limit prompts them to close the trial and record outstanding work.

**Service use and coaching.** Checkout can continue requesting ordinary one-app previews while both teams design the unsupported two-app case. They return that case to self-service once an engineer can run it from the revised instructions. In the coaching trial, a second Returns engineer demonstrates the team can perform and verify a rollback using its runbook. Returns then maintains that runbook.

**Conway’s law.** The first drawing relates a design conversation between teams to a possible dependency between their components. The linked Reverse Conway example begins with a desired reservation interface, then changes who owns and deploys the rule. Its pilot checks whether Stock can change the rule without a joint release.
